Do Affirmations Work? How to Write Ones That Actually Help

By the New.Vision editorial teamUpdated November 16, 20267 min read

What research says about self-affirmations, why some positive statements backfire, and a simple method for writing affirmations that feel believable and useful.

Key takeaways
  • Values-based self-affirmation has research support; extreme positive statements can backfire for some people.
  • Believable beats grandiose: use 'I am learning to…' or 'I choose to…'.
  • Pair an affirmation with an action to make it more than words.
In this guide
  1. What the research says
  2. Five rules for affirmations that help
  3. Weak vs strong affirmations
  4. A two-minute daily routine

What the research says

In psychology, "self-affirmation" usually means reflecting on your core values — not repeating slogans. Studies have linked values affirmation to reduced defensiveness and better responses to threatening information, and brain-imaging work has found that affirming core values activates areas associated with self-related processing and reward (Cascio et al., 2016).

But there's a twist. In a well-known study, people with low self-esteem who repeated "I am a lovable person" felt worse afterwards than those who didn't, while people with high self-esteem felt slightly better (Wood, Perunovic & Lee, 2009). When a statement clashes too strongly with what you believe, your mind argues back.

Five rules for affirmations that help

  1. Make it believable. "I'm getting stronger every week" beats "I'm the fittest person alive."
  2. Use process language. Try "I am learning to…", "I'm becoming someone who…", or "I choose to…".
  3. Anchor in values. "I value honesty, so I'll have the difficult conversation today."
  4. Be specific. Tie it to a goal on your vision board.
  5. Add an action. "I treat my body with care — so I'm going for a walk now."

Weak vs strong affirmations

Weak (can backfire)Stronger
I am rich.I make thoughtful money choices and save every payday.
I never feel anxious.I can feel anxious and still take the next step.
I'm perfect as I am.I'm worthy of care while I keep growing.
Success comes easily to me.I'm building skills that make success more likely.

A two-minute daily routine

  1. Read your top value and your vision statement.
  2. Say or write one believable affirmation linked to today's priority.
  3. Name the one action that proves it.

Frequently asked questions

How many times a day should I repeat affirmations?

There's no proven number. A short morning practice linked to an action is more useful than repetitive chanting.

Are affirmations a replacement for therapy?

No. If you're struggling with your mental health, please speak to a qualified professional.

References

  2. Wood, J. V., Perunovic, W. Q. E., & Lee, J. W. (2009). Positive self-statements: Power for some, peril for others. Psychological Science, 20(7), 860–866.
