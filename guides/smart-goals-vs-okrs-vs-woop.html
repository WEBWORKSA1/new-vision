Goal Setting

SMART Goals vs OKRs vs WOOP: Which Goal Framework Should You Use?

By the New.Vision editorial teamUpdated November 16, 20268 min read

Compare the three most popular goal-setting frameworks side by side, with examples, strengths, weaknesses and a simple way to choose.

Key takeaways
  • SMART is best for turning one fuzzy idea into a clear, testable goal.
  • OKRs suit ambitious, measurable outcomes — especially for teams and businesses.
  • WOOP is best when your real problem is follow-through, not clarity.

SMART goals

The SMART acronym was popularised by George T. Doran in a 1981 management article. Today it usually stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.

Example: "Save $6,000 for an emergency fund by 31 December by automatically transferring $500 on the 1st of each month."

Strengths: easy to learn, forces clarity, easy to check at the deadline. Weaknesses: "achievable" can push people toward safe goals; it says nothing about obstacles or motivation.

OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)

OKRs became famous through Intel and later Google, and were popularised by John Doerr's book Measure What Matters. An Objective is a qualitative, inspiring direction. Key Results are 2–5 measurable outcomes that prove you got there.

Example objective: "Launch a profitable side business." Key results: 1) 20 paying customers; 2) $2,000 monthly revenue; 3) 500 newsletter subscribers.

Strengths: encourages ambitious "stretch" thinking; great for aligning a team. Weaknesses: can be overkill for personal habits; needs regular check-ins to work.

WOOP

WOOP — Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan — was developed by psychologist Gabriele Oettingen from research on mental contrasting and implementation intentions. Its distinctive move is to make you name the inner obstacle and create an if-then plan to handle it.

Example: Wish: write a book draft. Outcome: holding the printed manuscript. Obstacle: I scroll my phone instead of writing. Plan: If I pick up my phone before 8am, then I'll put it in a drawer and write one paragraph.

Strengths: strong research base for follow-through; takes five minutes. Weaknesses: less suited to complex multi-person projects.

How to choose in 30 seconds

  • "I don't know exactly what I want" → start with SMART.
  • "I know what I want and it's big" → use OKRs.
  • "I know what to do, I just don't do it" → use WOOP.

You can also combine them — and that's exactly what our Goal Planner does: a SMART statement, quarterly milestones (a light version of key results) and a WOOP if-then plan in one place.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use OKRs for personal goals?

Yes. Keep it to one or two objectives per quarter, each with no more than three key results, and review weekly.

What does the A in SMART stand for?

Most commonly 'Achievable', though some versions use 'Assignable' or 'Ambitious'. Doran's original 1981 article used 'Assignable'.
