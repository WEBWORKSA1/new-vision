Vision Boards

How to Make a Vision Board That Actually Works (Step-by-Step)

By the New.Vision editorial teamUpdated November 16, 20269 min read

A practical, evidence-informed method for making a vision board you'll actually use — digital or paper — with prompts, layouts and a weekly review routine.

Key takeaways
  • A vision board works best as a planning tool, not a wish list: pair every image with a concrete next action.
  • Limit yourself to 6–9 goals across life areas so your attention isn't split.
  • Put the board where you see it daily and review it weekly for 5 minutes.

What a vision board really is

A vision board is a visual summary of the life you're working toward: images, words and symbols that represent specific goals. Done well, it becomes a daily cue that keeps your priorities in front of you when your calendar fills up with everyone else's. Done badly, it's a pretty collage that quietly gathers dust.

The difference is almost never artistic skill. It's whether the board is connected to decisions you make each week. This guide shows you how to build that connection.

Step 1 — Decide what the board is for

Before you open a single magazine or app, pick a time frame and a scope. Most people do best with a 12-month board that covers several life areas. If you're in the middle of a big transition — a new job, a move, a business launch — a focused 90-day board for that one area can be more powerful.

Write one sentence at the top of a notebook page: "By this time next year, my life looks like…" and finish it in as much detail as you can. That paragraph is your raw material.

Step 2 — Choose 6 to 9 goals across life areas

Run through these areas and note one meaningful goal for each that genuinely matters to you. Skip any that don't:

  • Career & work — a role, a project, a skill, a business milestone.
  • Money — an emergency fund, debt payoff, an investing habit, a purchase you're saving for.
  • Health — sleep, movement, food, energy.
  • Relationships, family & friends — time, rituals, repair, new connections.
  • Personal growth — learning, reading, a creative practice.
  • Fun & adventure — travel, hobbies, experiences.
  • Home & environment — where and how you live.

If you're not sure where to start, take the free Wheel of Life assessment first. The lowest-scoring areas usually deserve a spot.

Step 3 — Make each goal specific

"Be healthier" is a mood. "Run a 10K in under an hour by September" is a goal. Research on goal setting going back decades has found that specific, challenging goals tend to lead to better performance than vague "do your best" intentions (Locke & Latham, 2002). For each goal, write a short caption you could test at the end of the year. Our Goal Planner turns any idea into a SMART goal in about two minutes.

Step 4 — Find images that create a feeling

Choose images that make you feel something when you look at them — not just pictures of objects. A photo of a calm morning kitchen can represent a healthier routine better than a picture of a kale smoothie. Good sources include your own photos, free stock libraries such as Unsplash and Pexels, printed magazines, and quick sketches. Add words: a one-line caption, a date, or a short affirmation.

Step 5 — Lay it out

A simple grid beats a chaotic collage for most people because each tile is clearly one goal. Put your single most important goal in the centre or top-left, where your eye lands first. Use the free New.Vision board maker to build a 6, 9 or 12-tile board, drag tiles to rearrange them, and export a high-resolution PNG you can print or set as your phone wallpaper.

Step 6 — Attach one next action to every tile

This is the step most guides skip. Researchers who study "mental contrasting" have found that simply fantasising about a positive future can actually reduce the energy you put into pursuing it (Kappes & Oettingen, 2011). What helps is pairing the vision with the obstacle and a plan. On the back of the board, or in a note, write for every tile: "The next thing I'll do is ___ by ___."

Step 7 — Display it and review it weekly

Place the board somewhere you'll see it every day: your lock screen, the inside of a wardrobe door, beside your desk. Then set a recurring 5-minute weekly review. Ask three questions: What moved? What's stuck? What's the next action? Swap out tiles that no longer excite you — a vision board is a living document.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Too many goals. Twenty goals means no goals. Cap it at nine.
  • Only "having" goals. Balance things you want to own with who you want to become and what you want to experience.
  • Borrowed goals. If a goal is there because someone else expects it, remove it.
  • Set and forget. Without a review rhythm the board fades into wallpaper within weeks.

Frequently asked questions

How many pictures should a vision board have?

Aim for one image per goal and 6–9 goals in total. More than that dilutes your focus.

Is a digital vision board as good as a paper one?

Yes, if you see it often. A digital board on your phone lock screen may be seen 50+ times a day, which is an advantage.

When is the best time to make a vision board?

Any time you want a fresh start: New Year, a birthday, a new job or a new season. Psychologists call this the 'fresh start effect' (Dai, Milkman & Riis, 2014).

References
